How the calendar works

Why so many celebrations?

Ancient Iranian life followed the seasons. Farming, water, fire, plants, animals, family memory, and the renewal of the world all had places in the calendar. In the Zoroastrian calendar, each day and each month has a dedication. When a day name matches its month name, the result is a jashn: a gathering of worship, thanksgiving, shared food, and joy.

Not every celebration below is the same kind of tradition. Some are Zoroastrian religious observances; some are national or seasonal customs shared by Iranians of many beliefs; and some developed later. Dates can also differ between Fasli/Bastani, Shahenshahi, Qadimi, and modern Solar Hijri calendars.

Scope: This page covers the principal calendar-wide festivals and the best-known national celebrations. Ancient Iran also had many local observances—too many for any single list to claim literally “all.”

Solar Hijri order

The celebration calendar

Dates below use familiar Iranian month/day notation. Gregorian dates vary slightly by year and community.

01

Farvardin

Spring · renewal

1 Farvardin

Nowruz

New Year · spring equinox

Iran’s best-known festival welcomes spring with house-cleaning, new clothes, visits, gifts, a Haft-Sin table, and shared meals. In Zoroastrian tradition it is the holiest annual festival and celebrates the renewal of creation.

Theology: renewal, creation, truth, and the hoped-for final restoration of the world.

Shahnameh: Jamshid is raised on a jeweled throne and shines like the sun; people call the radiant day “Nowruz.”

13 Farvardin

Sizdah Bedar

Nature Day

Families spend the thirteenth day of Nowruz outdoors, picnic, play games, and return the growing sabzeh to nature. It closes the modern Nowruz holiday cycle.

Meaning: a national nature custom rather than a core Zoroastrian name-day feast.

19 Farvardin

Farvardingan

Fravashi name-day

A day honoring the fravashis—protective spiritual essences associated with the righteous and the remembered dead. Families may pray, remember loved ones, and share blessed food.

Theology: memory, moral guardianship, and continuity between generations.

02–03

Ordibehesht & Khordad

Truth · water

3 Ordibehesht

Ordibeheshtgan

Honors Asha Vahishta—“Best Truth” or perfect order—and its created symbol, fire. The day invites honesty, right action, and care for pure fire.

Theology: truth, justice, cosmic order, and transforming light.

6 Khordad

Khordadgan

Honors Haurvatat/Khordad, wholeness and well-being, traditionally guardian of water. Clean water and gratitude for life are central ideas.

Theology: completeness, health, abundance, and protection of water.

04

Tir

Rain · summer

13 Tir

Tirgan

Water and rain festival

Dedicated to Tishtrya, the rain-bringing star. Customs include joyful water-splashing and colorful wrist ribbons. It belongs to Iran’s great seasonal festivals.

Theology: life-giving rain, the defeat of drought, and responsible care for water.

Epic tradition: Arash the Archer gives all his strength to a single arrow that sets Iran’s border. The shot is traditionally dated to Tirgan; the full tale is better preserved outside Ferdowsi’s surviving Shahnameh narrative.

05–06

Amordad & Shahrivar

Plants · just rule

7 Amordad

Amordadgan

Celebrates Ameretat/Amordad, “Immortality,” guardian of plants. Gardens, greenery, and the food plants give humanity are natural themes.

Theology: long life, immortality, nourishment, and protection of plant life.

4 Shahrivar

Shahrivargan

Honors Khshathra Vairya, “Desirable Dominion,” linked with metals. Its lesson is that real power should serve justice and protect the vulnerable.

Theology: good governance, moral strength, service, and responsible use of material power.

07

Mehr

Harvest · covenant

16 Mehr

Mehregan

Great autumn festival

Dedicated to Mithra/Mehr, guardian of covenants, truth, friendship, and watchfulness. Today it is celebrated with prayer, music, a decorated sofreh, autumn fruit, and community meals.

Theology: keeping promises, fairness, loyal friendship, sunlight, and gratitude at harvest.

Shahnameh connection: Fereydun defeats the tyrant Zahhak; later Iranian tradition associates the victory of justice with Mehregan.

08–09

Aban & Azar

Water · fire

10 Aban

Abanegan

A name-day feast of the Waters, often associated with the powerful yazata Anahita. Celebrants express gratitude for clean rivers, rain, and the water that sustains every community.

Theology: purity, fertility, strength, and reverence for water.

9 Azar

Azargan

Honors Atar, sacred fire. Fire is not worshipped as a god; it is treated as a pure, visible focus for truth, clarity, and the presence of divine order.

Theology: purity, insight, moral energy, and protection of fire from pollution.

Night of 30 Azar

Yalda / Shab-e Chelleh

Winter solstice

Families stay together through the longest night with lamps, poetry, stories, nuts, watermelon, and pomegranate. The name “Yalda” is Syriac, while the Iranian solstice customs celebrate warmth and the return of longer days.

Meaning: a national solstice tradition, often interpreted through light overcoming the deepest darkness—not a classical month-name jashn.

Literature: families often read Hafez and tell Shahnameh stories, though Yalda has no single founding episode in the Shahnameh.

10

Dey

The Creator

1, 8, 15 & 23 Dey

Deygan

Dey means “Creator” and is an epithet of Ahura Mazda. Because three additional days each month also carry the Dey dedication, the month contains several creator-honoring observances.

Theology: wisdom, creation, generosity, and the unity of the good creation.

11

Bahman

Good mind · fire

2 Bahman

Bahmangan

Honors Vohu Manah/Bahman, the “Good Mind,” and the beneficent animal creation—especially cattle in the older pastoral world.

Theology: compassionate thought, wise choices, and humane care for animals.

10 Bahman

Sadeh

Midwinter fire festival

A great community bonfire marks winter’s turning and anticipates Nowruz. The fire expresses warmth, cooperation, and the human ability to push back cold and darkness.

Meaning: a major Iranian/Zoroastrian winter fire festival, though it is not named in the surviving Avesta.

Shahnameh: a popular passage credits King Hushang with discovering fire when a thrown stone strikes rock and sparks; scholars regard this specific Sadeh origin story as a later addition.

12

Esfand

Earth · year’s end

5 Esfand

Sepandarmazgan

Honors Spenta Armaiti, beneficent devotion and guardian of earth. Historically it became a festival of farmers and women; modern celebrations also emphasize love and appreciation.

Theology: humility, patient devotion, the nurturing earth, and responsible farming.

Last Wednesday eve

Chaharshanbe Suri

People jump over small bonfires, make noise, and welcome the final days before Nowruz. The Wednesday-based form developed after antiquity, although its use of cleansing fire resonates with older Iranian symbolism.

Meaning: a national pre-Nowruz custom; it should not be presented as an unchanged ancient Zoroastrian rite.

Final 10 days

Frawardigan

A year-end period honoring the fravashis and the departed. Homes and places of worship are prepared to welcome memory, prayer, and generosity before Nowruz.

Theology: the living community remembers the righteous dead and prepares creation for renewal.

Jashn-ha-ye Gahambari

The six seasonal creation festivals

Along with Nowruz, the six Gahambars form the seven Zoroastrian feasts of obligation. Each is a multi-day season of prayer, equality, hospitality, and shared food.

Mid-spring

Maidyozarem

Celebrates the creation of the sky.

Midsummer

Maidyoshahem

Celebrates the creation of water.

Harvest

Paitishahem

Celebrates the creation of earth.

Herds return

Ayathrem

Celebrates the creation of plants.

Midwinter

Maidyarem

Celebrates the animal creation.

Year’s end

Hamaspathmaedaya

Celebrates humanity and flows into Frawardigan.

Taste the calendar

Food traditions, kept simple

Menus vary by region and family. These are approachable modern examples, not claims of one universal ancient recipe.

Yalda

Pomegranate & walnut bowl

  1. Seed 2 pomegranates.
  2. Add toasted walnuts and a pinch of golpar.
  3. Finish with a little rosewater or lime, if desired.

Serve beside watermelon, dried fruit, and mixed nuts—the red fruit symbolizes warmth and life.

Yalda

Easy labu

  1. Wash whole beets and cover with water.
  2. Simmer until tender, then peel and slice.
  3. Return to the pan with a little cooking liquid and optional sugar.

Serve warm; UNESCO lists beetroot among Yalda’s symbolic red foods.

Mehregan

Autumn saffron rice

  1. Cook and drain basmati rice until just tender.
  2. Steam with saffron water.
  3. Top with pistachios, almonds, barberries, and orange peel.

Pair with seasonal grapes, pomegranate, apples, nuts, sweets, and rosewater on a Mehregan spread.

Nowruz

Sabzi polo

  1. Mix parboiled basmati rice with chopped dill, parsley, cilantro, and chives.
  2. Steam until fluffy.
  3. Serve with fish or a vegetarian centerpiece.

Fresh herbs make spring renewal visible on the table.
